# Creative production

Use this workflow when shaping or producing a visual project where both the creative direction and production method matter. For detailed checks, continue with [creative-web-production](../creative-web-production/SKILL.md) for websites or [shortform-video-production](../shortform-video-production/SKILL.md) for short videos.

## Learn from experiments without inheriting their look

- Recover the current brief, project handoff and the user's own review. If relevant experiment records are available, consult them for the reason a result worked, what held it back, and the technique or asset lesson. Keep the user's judgment distinct from supervisor inference and automated checks.
- Reuse proven working methods, not a house style. Prior approval of one visual idea does not approve its reuse everywhere. Do not make animation, component motion, characters, 3D or a particular renderer the default.
- For open-ended work, offer genuinely different creative directions when that choice would affect the result. Vary the underlying idea, visual language or storytelling method; cosmetic versions of one template do not count. Let the user choose when the decision is theirs, and keep moving on independent research or preparation.
- Research current references, styles, tools, access and rights when they materially affect the brief. Say what was actually inspected (for example, a live video, sampled frames, stills or text); do not imply a fuller review than was done.

## Make the intended quality achievable

- Turn the brief into an audience, intended effect, content or story, and visible quality criteria. Preserve settled facts, caveats, personal preferences and open decisions.
- Before visual implementation, match the desired result to usable assets and a real production method. A concept image can guide art direction but may not supply controllable layers, geometry, footage or motion. Do not quietly replace a premium physical-object shot with a hand-drawn approximation; explain the gap and propose viable options.
- Compare methods against the brief, access, skill, time, editability, licensing and reviewability. Choose the simplest method that can reach the intended quality; experimentation is useful when it tests a real creative or technical question.
- Make a concrete, reviewable draft early enough to learn from it. Review the rendered experience, not only code or a storyboard. Use the user's response to make focused revisions, preserving versions when comparison is useful.

## Review and carry learning forward

- Verify the qualities that can be checked: factual or content accuracy, responsive behavior, readability, interactions, playback, sound, accessibility, performance and licensing as appropriate to the medium. Use browser, visual, audio and media tools that are available and appropriate; distinguish observed evidence from assumptions.
- Leave subjective judgments such as personality, taste, pacing and voice quality to human review. Measurements can inform that review but cannot replace it.
- Close each experiment with a short record in the project: objective, chosen direction and method, source and asset provenance, what was actually reviewed, results, user feedback, limitations, and the next decision. Preserve the source and useful versions.
- When a shared knowledge workspace is available and the user wants continuity, record the concise lesson and link to project evidence there. Promote a lesson into a Skill only when it changes future decisions beyond that one project; keep project facts and long evidence in the repository.
